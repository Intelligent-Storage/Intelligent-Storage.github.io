<h2 style="text-align: center;"> Propper International FILBE vs Savotta Kahakka 25L<br> America vs Europe </h2>

<div style="margin: 2rem 0; padding: 2rem; background: linear-gradient(135deg, rgba(99, 179, 237, 0.08), rgba(139, 92, 246, 0.08)); border-radius: 8px; border-left: 4px solid #63b3ed;">

<p><b>Article Description:</b> This is an in-depth gear review of two "military" backpacks (American and Finnish). From here on, it'll get more technical and critical. You've been warned.</p>

<p>This is a comparative design and quality review of the <a href="https://www.propper.com/usmc-assault-pack.html">FILBE Assault Pack (AP)</a>, used
by the United States Marine Corps (USMC), and the <a href="https://www.savotta.fi/products/Kahakka-25l">Savotta Kahakka 25L Backpack</a>, a commercial
version of the <a href="https://puolustusvoimat.fi/en/equipment/-/cat/asset/view/id/1905">M20 pack</a> used by Finnish Defense Forces (FDF).</p>

<b> This review is not about which pack is superior, nor about providing 
ratings -- it is about understanding the design, engineering, and build quality
of these packs and how they could be improved. I attempt to account for the
reasoning behind the design decisions and if the justifications are warranted. </b>

</div>

<p style="text-align: center;"><b>How do two military assault packs, <br> one from the USA's defense powerhouse, <Br> the other born from Finland's sisu, <br> measure up?</b></p>
<br>

While characterizing this as an <span class="footnote-ref"> "Europe vs America" is a bit unfair,<span class="footnote-tooltip"> It may be unfair because: <br> 1)The Kahakka is made for commercial, not military sale <br> 2) Different manufactures (Propper vs Eagle Industries) may have differing qualities, independent of home country <br> 3) Propper and Savotta don't make all the backpacks (or equipment) for all of American and Finish armed forces. </span></span>
it is still worthwhile to compare the design considerations and build quality between products fulfilling similar roles across the Atlantic.
Some of the noted findings do seem to highlight the cultural and stereotypical differences between the countries (USA vs Finland) in question.

<b>Testing duration:</b> Both of these backpacks have been tested for at least 1 year.

<b>Relevant Experience:</b> I have never been in combat. I have played a lot of milsim sports. I have solid engineering and user design experience.

<b>The principal considerations are:</b>

1. Everyday Carry Use (EDC)
2. Combat Usage (CU)
3. Manufacturing and Quality

I will refer to each pack as the <span class="footnote-ref"> FILBE <span class="footnote-tooltip">Typically, FILBE refers to the main rucksack (80L) in the FILBE system, not the assault pack. Here I will just refer to it as the FILBE.</span></span>and Kahakka from here on.

## Summary of differences (TLDR)

<div style="margin: 2rem 0; padding: 2rem; background: linear-gradient(135deg, rgba(99, 179, 237, 0.08), rgba(139, 92, 246, 0.08)); border-radius: 8px; border-left: 4px solid #63b3ed;">

<p> <span class="footnote-ref"> The FILBE is bigger and heavier,<span class="footnote-tooltip"> The FILBE has a larger volume, even if the tech specs say otherwise.</span></span> capturing the American design ethos. The Kahakka
is smaller and leaner, demonstrating the European one.
</p>

<p> For everyday and short-term combat usage, the <i>design</i> of the FILBE is superior. The FILBE's
simple compartment system ensures quick and effective organization. Its J-Zip provides
great ease-of-access. In contrast, the Kahakka's top flap closure is inconvenient, especially for items
at the bottom of the pack. The hunter's green PU inner lining makes visibility in the compartment
difficult. For long term combat usage and poor weather, the 
Kahakka's design <i>may</i> be better because of improved defense against the elements,
excepting a lack of drainage grommets.
</p>

<p>The FILBE is more comfortable with heavier loads due to wider, better padded straps. It is
cooler on the back because of the dual-channel padding. This advantage is negated
while wearing a plate carrier. For travel, the Kahakka is 0.5 kg lighter. Notably, 
the actual body height is only 40cm (not the advertised 48cm), meaning if not overstuffed, it meets RyanAir's 
small bag dimensions (40x30x20cm). The FILBE (~51 cm) will require bending if tested.
</p>


<p>While aesthetics are mostly opinion, it can affect every day carry and how others
react to you. The FILBE clearly carries a military air,
while the Kahakka less so. I have been prevented from entering certain museums
and areas of interest while wearing the FILBE, although this was during the
European Terror waves (2015-2017). I think it is less
likely I would have been stopped with the Kahakka, but still possible.
</p>

<p>The <i>quality</i> of the Kahakka is simply superior - both in terms of material
 and build. The manufacturing quality of the FILBE has, in my
limited experience, significantly regressed since its release.
</p>


<p>For EDC, where I must access items readily, the FILBE is the clear choice. For hiking,
the decision comes down to season, and carry needs. In hot weather or heavy carry loads,
the FILBE is my choice. For cold weather, the choice is equivalent. For general travel
where size and weight matters, the <span class="footnote-ref">Kahakka is better<span class="footnote-tooltip">The exception to this is if you have a lot of computer equipment to carry, and must it removed at airport security. Item access with the FILBE is just so much better.</span></span>.
</p>

<b>As of 02/2026, I am working on a redesign of my Kahakka based on my experiences,
most of which are noted here. I intend to bring it to a tailor for modifications,
making it more practical. You can find [my project details here](/projects/gear/Kahakka-mailli-1).</b>

</div>

## Introduction and History

If you're here, you likely already have an interest in at least one of these bags.
Therefore, I'll be concise with the history.

The Family of Improved Load Bearing Equipment (FILBE) Assault Pack (AP) was first fielded
by the USMC sometime in [2011-2012](https://www.militarytimes.com/off-duty/gearscout/kit/2020/02/11/looking-for-a-kickass-new-backpack-consider-the-tried-and-true-marine-corps-filbe/).
As of 02/2026 (time of writing), it is still the AP in service for the USMC. It was produced by both
Eagle Industries and Propper International. Only in late 2024 did it become available for
consumer purchase from Propper. However, it was available as military surplus as early
as 2013-2014.

The Kahakka was released to the consumer market in 2024, making it some 12 years younger. It is based on
the the FDF's M20 pack, [with learnings from the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine.](https://www.savotta.fi/pages/Kahakka-backpack-origins)

## Why I care so much about this (and why it matters to you!)

I had the same backpack for 9 years (RIP 2015-2024). It was used at least as many days as I've had it, probably more. 

Sometimes it hardly carried anything but papers, other times it was stuffed to 
the brim, camping gear strapped to the side. It was with me as I rode the métro 
and walked the streets of Paris to university. It was with me when I reached the 
foot of the glaciers on Mont Blanc. It's seen the beaches on both sides of the 
Atlantic and the Mediterranean. It's been over the Alps, Appalachians, Blue 
Stacks and Derryveaghs, and even graced the Lake District. It's survived a 
dozen different farms, carried books into at least 4 different universities, 
and *most importantly*, had a pocket just for holding a fresh baguette.

<br>
<p style="text-align: center;"><b>That pack was the FILBE</b></p>
<br>

When you've carried something like that for so long, it becomes a part of you, 
and an icon of yourself. Folks recognize you just by its silhouette. When I 
tested other bags, my friends didn't like it. "You should get a new one of the 
same bag. It's like it's not you anymore," they said.


<br>
<p style="text-align: center;"><b>And, I didn't <i>really</i> want a new bag</b></p>
<br>

Unfortunately, after 9 years of wear-and-tear, my original FILBE needed to be
replaced. It wasn't so much the standard usage which got it, but an accident within
the first months of purchase. I left it against a radiator in a caravan 
(that wasn't supposed to work), waking during the middle of the night to the 
odor of burning plastic. The melted holes became weak points no patching seemed 
to resolve. Eventually, one huge tear formed at the top of the bag, perpetually 
letting in rain.

<p style="text-align: center;"><b>It was time for a new bag - but which?</b></p>
<br>

Whatever replacement pack came, it would be would have big shoes to 
fill. My FILBE had been part of my life for just shy of a decade. I knew it inside
and out.

Buying a new one seemed the obvious choice. But, a new FILBE direct from Propper
would cost $180, a frustrating price given how much I originally paid. 
Used ones could be had for much less. "Like-new" ones however
were not acceptably cheaper to risk getting <span class="footnote-ref">one with holes<span class="footnote-tooltip">It was not possible at the time to physically visit a military surplus store that might have one for physical inspection.</span></span>, likely leading to the
same demise as the previous. 

So, I cared a lot about getting a new pack, and the right one. And it matters because
whatever pack would come next would have to meet high standards.

<br>
<p style="text-align: center;"><b>Enter the Kahakka</b></p>
<br>

At about this time, I was interested in [The Road to Vostok](https://www.roadtovostok.com/),

Savotta.

**[Savotta](https://www.savotta.fi/)** is a well-known Finnish supplier of outdoor gear, for both military, 
police, and recreational use in "extreme-environments."

Traveling between the USA and Europe tends to result in what I call 
<b>"[aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_(image)#:~:text=The%20aspect%20ratio%20of%20an,3%3A2%20in%20still%20photography.) shock."</b>
— a sensation that everything has been visually stretched. It's akin to changing the 
aspect ratio on your TV or monitor from 4:3 to 16:10 in an instant (or vice versa) - 
just with real life.


Go from Europe to America and the world feels flattened and stretched wide. A
"single lane" in American suburbia feels equivalent to a rural
double lane in the European countryside.
Come back to continental Europe and everything is tall and narrow, from 
ceiling heights to light posts. More generally, American products 
are <span class="hover-note" id="note1">heavier, bulkier, and more comfortable. European
products are lighter, more economical, and spartan.</span>

<div class="hover-box" id="box1">
There are a lot of examples of this aspect-ratio and product differences between
Europe and the USA. Consider the difference between American and European
printing paper formats. Americans love
their "short and fat" 8.5″x10″ sheets, and Europeans their 8.3″x11.7″.
<br><br>
Regarding products, cars are a perfect example, even from the same manufacturer.
American cars are larger. Even when comparing the same car model, just between
continents, American suspensions are softer. While European products are
trimmed, this also means they're fitted.
<br><br>
You can also observe this in how the average person dresses in Europe versus the USA.
Europeans are better dressed. They tend to wear tight, hemmed clothes,
pants that aren't jeans, and aren't necessarily blue (or black or khaki),
sweaters over their button up long-sleeve shirts, and nice shoes.
Each pair of socks they own are unique. Not dozens of pairs of the same
exact sock. And well… Americans, they wear that one-size-fits-all from Costco,
baggy jeans, and if they're an older father, those "classic" 608 V5s (because well, <i>they are</i> comfortable).
<br><br>
<i>As an experiment, I gave Nano Banana Pro the following image generation prompt:
"Imagine me stereotypical images of American man average clothing and average European"</i>
<br><br>

## Overview

Each aspect of the packs will be reviewed and compared in this section 
(i.e. comparments, buckles, zippers, etc.). The main considerations are
for EDC (Everyday Carry) and Combat Use (CU). Build quality is discussed later,
but mentioned where specifically appropriate. 

Two terms worth highlighting are <b>Single Handed Usage (SHU)</b> and <b>Fine Motor Control (FMC)</b>.
These are important concepts in military applications (to me) because they 
determine how readily a product can be used when injured (single handed usage case) 
and/or when under extreme stress where fine motor control becomes reduced.


### Main Compartment

#### Main Compartment Access
The Main Compartment is the heart of every pack. Access to the main compartment 
on the Kahakka is through a fold top lid secured by 2 side release (SR) buckles. 
The female ends of these buckles are note worthy because they have flared entrances, 
similar to a [flared magwell](https://gunmagwarehouse.com/blog/the-reasoning-behind-flared-magwells/#:~:text=Well%2C%20it%20has%20a%20larger,truly%20as%20simple%20as%20that.). 
This is the first time I've seen this on an SR buckle, though I'm sure they exist elsewhere.
It's a strong design choice - it does make buckling easier, especially in
stressful situations where fine motor control goes out this window.

In comparison, the FILBE uses a J-zip, so named because the zipper is stretches 
longer down one side than the other.

In terms of opening the main compartment, it might be argued that the Kahakka is
easier. It only requires one hand to unlatch the buckles, and then lift the lid.
The FILBE has two zippers, but they do not slide freely enough without some
additional force on the bag to keep it in place. Therefore, one handed usage is
easier on the Kahakka, but accessing specific items is more challenging.

Unbuckle the Kahakka's main SR clips and the fold top lid extends open. This system
is common in many recreational hiking packs. Fold top lids are great because they
eliminate the weaknesses of zippers while leveraging the pack's shell to keep out
the elements. But for an assault pack, I'm not convinced they are the right choice.

While opening can be done single handed, this system doesn't provide
easy access to materials in the compartments, except those placed at the top. When
the fold top lid opens, the folding portion becomes an extended neck. In
effect, the pack becomes a longer tube. Bottom access is challenging. My arm length
is typical to that of a 6' or 183cm tall man. As an EDC example, putting the 
Kahakka on cashier's counter and trying to put in groceries in an organized manner
to maximize space usage and not crush anything, is actually really hard. I ultimately
stopped using the Kahakka as an EDC mainly for this reason. I couldn't get my
<span class="footnote-ref">groceries without struggling.<span class="footnote-tooltip">And we know what those Aldi/Lidl cashiers are like!</span></span>

One solution to this is often found in larger hiking packs with fold top lids. At
the bottom, there is a zippered "sleeping bag section." However, when everything
must be packed from bottom-to-top, things will inevitably get pulled out of the 
pack onto the ground for access. I can't imagine having to fidget with any of this
, fold top lid or even a sleeping bag zipper, in combat when some sort of 
ammunition, medical supply, etc. has made its way to the bottom or some unknown
position. For this, the J-Zip on the FILBE is far more convenient.

However, one redemption of the implementation in the Kahakka's fold top lid is a
plastic insert in the lower lip (towards bottom of picture below). This little
flexible piece does the job of keeping the mouth of the lid open and <span class="footnote-ref">squared.<span class="footnote-tooltip"> I know it's a rectangle, but "rectangled" doesn't read as smoothly as a verb </span></span>
There is however a seam on this lower lip which is sharp and seems to catch a lot.
I was concerned how this seam would hold up in time, but after more than a year it
is no worse for wear.

#### Main Compartment Organization

The Kahakka has limited organization in the main compartment - a single pouch 
for a hydration bladder. The top of the pouch is kept under tension by an internal
elastic band. There are 3 togs providing anchors to a bladder, though only one is
visible in my photo.

I am not a fan of elastic tensioning because these materials eventually "dry out,"
losing their elasticity and ability to do their job.

There is one particular grievances I have against the Kahakka - its internal PU 
coating. This is a great idea to increase weather resistance. But, this particular
coating is a hunter's green (i.e. very dark green). I know not if this was intentional,
such as a carry over from procurement specifications for the M20. For example,
it might be a deliberate decision to reduce any potential of an offending light
signature leaking from inside the main compartment at night. But ultimately, 
this dark coating makes locating anything, even on a sunny day, frustrating. 

My constructive advice would have be to use a lighter material to reflect light
further down the cavity, and if needed, use a darker coating only at the very top,
and/or on the outer pouches, for concealment.

In comparison, the FILBE has simple but more developed organization. A zippered
mesh pocket hands at the top front. It is very easy to access, even just opening the main J-Zip
slightly, and allows you to see what's inside due to the mesh covering. I have 
found this particularly useful for EDC and travel for items like keys and IDs.

The FILBE features a radio comparment, which can be used to hold
a hydration bladder. Unlike the Kahakka, it does not have locking anchors for the
bladder, presumably because these would get in the way of the radio. In my own
experience with the FILBE and a water bladder, the lack of anchors makes little
difference. The radio pouch features a strap with buckle, something which is
preferred over the elastic of the Kahakka's elastic band (the buckle is not
going to wear out).

Both the Kahakka and FILBE have exit slots for water bladder tubes (or radio 
antennae for the later). I heavily prefer the flap coverings that Savotta has used. 
They are located on each side of the flip top lid, just above the hemming in my 
photos, towards the rear of the packs. The exit slots on the FILBE are covered 
by Velcro and part of the top carrying handle. Despite rarely using these, they 
show great wear at this stage. Their top placement makes them less than ideal 
for inclement weather, but this may be a necessity for the intended radios antennas.

The FILBE also features 2 internal side pockets or "canteen" pouches which I
have personally named as follows. One is the "Baguette holder", a long pocket
tube seen in the folder below. The second 
is the "Grenade holder" on the other side. It's not clear to me what either is 
explicitly for. The Baguette holder, besides holding baguettes, is useful for 
cables, chargers, and dongles as an EDC. Speaking of baguettes, another benefit 
of the J zipper, is being able to partially close the pack, while having extended 
objects secured in the main compartment (like a baguette). As for the Grenade holder,
I've found little utility for it except for rolled socks. 

Finally, the main compartment also features a zipper for access to the plastic 
rear panel, just above the radio pouch. This is actually an additional
"compartment." In the past I've used this pouch to store documents and 
hide back-up cash while traveling as the zipper goes unnoticed. It is strange
that a zipper was incorporated here instead of a flap, or even no opening at all
as it would've likely lowered production cost and weight.

One final note of the main compartment - the Kahakka emplys no drainage grommets
while the FILBE utilizes 3 (and 2 for the front pouch). Drainage grommets permit water to escape should it
manage to get inside. The Finns and the Marines are focued on rather different 
geographic sectors. The lack of grommets may be due to the Finnish Defense Forces pack requirements, 
which again, I am wholly ignorant of, and they may have requested no external
openings for drainage. The Finns may prefer the the "Hardshell" philosophy -
"Never give water a chance to get in, and you won't get wet."

On the otherhand, with the USMC being focused on maritime operations, and taking
the "Softshell" philosophy - "You will get wet no matter what, so keep the door
open" - it's no surprise that even their [hot weather boot](https://www.bellevilleboot.com/index.php?l=product_detail&p=96) 
has drainage grommets.

### Pouches and Pockets

#### Kahakka

The Kahakka has 2 external zippered pockets, and 2 external side pouches. The 
first external pocket is part of the folding lid and can be accessed by a 
double-slider zipper. This zipper has a fabric overhang providing weather 
protection. The pocket is described as having a 
"a capacity of ca. 3 litres, suitable for NVDs, water bottles etc."

However, this estimation seems unrealistic as the pocket is quite small. I doubt a
2L bottle would fit within. Even without gloves, my hand is always scrapping
against the zippers when trying to retrieve items. I imagine this would only be
more challenging while wearing gloves during the Finnish winter. 

I believe this access and volume issue stems from the shape of the pocket, and 
its shared wall which forms the top of the lid. Tension on the lid from when 
the pack is closed can make storage and retrieval harder.

The promotional video for the [Kahakka 25L](https://youtu.be/M3rsKZi5hmE?t=15) 
shows them pulling out a pair of binoculars. The key to accessing the
compartment is therefore by using an additional hand to pull the external layer 
and top <span class="footnote-ref">stringer<span class="footnote-tooltip">A <i>stringer</i> is the assembly of one side of a zipper. That is, the fabric tape and the attached teeth for either the top or bottom row.</span></span> upward. This means single handed use is more difficult.

<div style="text-align: center;">

At first I was quite nervous about putting anything small or important inside, 
such as keys or ID, because the zipper is close to the bottom of the pocket, and my
initial testing found items were likely to spill out. My +1 year of experience
though has found this is be less probable than on first appearance, but one must
remain mindful. It is the easiest pocket to access on the Kahakka but the 
least secured. 

Personally, I would like to see the zipper moved 2-3cm (~1") higher.
This would mean the fabric overhang would start just at the top of the velcro 
patch on the lid.

For those wondering, the [Medium sized Savotta Rain Cover](https://www.savotta.fi/products/backpack-cover) 
fits perfectlly inside this compartment but consumes nearly all the volume. 
You can still put some minor items in, but
at the risk of them being pulled out unintentionally when retrieving the cover.
As a side note, the Savotta's
rain cover fits the Kahakka quite well, though it is a big baggy. This bagginess
does however mean it will comfortably cover the pack, even with fully utilized side
pouches. Savotta now sells a 30L rain cover which would be more ideal for those interested
in a slim line look (or for infrared rating).


The second zipper pocket is a <span class="footnote-ref">flat pocket<span class="footnote-tooltip">A flat pocket is one which does not contain its own volume, but consumes volume from another compartment. In this case, the volume comes from the main compartment. </span></span> on the front of the pack. 

The flat pocket also employs a 2-way zipper (i.e. double-slided zipper), and features the same
fabric overhang for weather proofing. It too is difficult to access. If the 
top lid is buckled closed, access is not permitted. This can be considered a good
thing if you want a pocket with additional security. A D-Ring inside is provided
for attaching items (like keys) using a carabiner or other clipping system. 
The pocket can <i>just</i> fit a Kindle Scribe (1st generation 19.6cm x 22.9cm, or 7.72″x9.06″).

Because this is a flat pocket, it fights for space with whatever is in the main
compartment. If the main compartment is full, it will be difficult to insert or
remove anything from the flat pocket.

Finally, there are the Kahakka's "famous" side pouches. I say famous because

I like them, even if in some sense they go against my ideals of organization.

asking to be filled with water. For the land of a thousand lakes, I don't think
water capture is a necessary feature in the pack.

#### FILBE 

The FILBE features a 2-way zipper pocket on the lower front, with an estimated
volume of 6 liters. The zipper, like on the main compartment, is protected with 
a zipper gasket (a rubberized coating over the teeth). Inside, there is a mesh 
pockets at the top with a 1-way zipper.

The pocket is large enough for several books, and/or tablet, and/or large laptop
chargers, etc. I often used this to hold water as well, as it is easier access 
than the main compartment when using bottles instead of a bladder.

I wouldn't change a thing about this, except maybe to make the mesh pocket have
a double-slider. I just want to reiterate, it is a deceptively small (i.e. seems
small but is surprising large), and I am always impressed just how much can be
fit inside.

### Shoulder Straps and Buckles

The FILBE and Kahakka have different size and shape shoulder straps. The FILBE is,
in accordance to American designs, wider and thicker. Compared with the Kahakka,
the FILBE's straps are 12mm (0.5") wider, and 2.5mm (0.1") thicker.


<div style="text-align: center;">
  <img src="/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_140026.jpg" alt="Kahakka Strap on top of FILBE strap" style="max-width: 75%; height: auto; display: block; margin: 0 auto;">
  <p style="margin-top: 5px; font-size: 0.9em; color: var(--dark-olive); font-style: italic;">Kahakka Strap on top of FILBE strap</p>

The Kahakka straps are approximately 60 mm wide (2.26", measured at the widest
point) whereas, the FILBE is approximately 72 mm (2.83"). The Kahakka's straps 
are roughly 9.5mm (0.37") thick while the FILBE's are 12mm (0.47"). 


In terms of comfort, both are good, but the FILBE's extra width and padding 
provides it an edge. I can't answer at the time of writing this section 
the difference in comfort while wearing a plate carrier.

One of the Kahakka's features are the use of a D-Ring for the shoulder strap
attachment. While this doesn't really allow for true width adjustment of the 
straps, it can offer some relief in this aspect. In comparison the FILBE's straps
are completely fixed.

I imagined the difference this D-ring would make in comfort was going to be
greater than what I experienced on arrival and with further testing. But as an average 
sized male, I simply didn't notice any improvement in comfort. However, it can
be seen that for larger individuals this may make the Kahakka more comfortable 
as the fixed, non-rotating width between straps of the FILBE might be too narrow
for those with wide backs or thick necks. I did find that getting into and out 
of the backpack is slightly easier with the Kahakka with this rotation feature 
as the straps can be swung to a more convenient position.

One major benefit that the FILBE has over the Kahakka (and notably its parent, 
the M20) is the shoulder strap's quick detach mechanism. The padded straps
terminate with QR buckles, allowing the user to instantly remove the backpack. 
This can be done while in the prone position (or virtually any other), without
the user having to rotate or move anything but their hand to the QR buckles.
It also means a second person can easily remove the pack from the wearer, such
as in a wounded situation, with just two pinches. Should the QR buckles, which
also serve as adjusters for the strap length, break, the FILBE comes with 
back-up, fixed connections. 

Given the Kahakka is a commercial pack it's understandable such a feature isn't
included. However, as far as I can ascertain from the few published photos, 
the M20 doesn't include this quick detach feature, and I think it is
certainly worth while.

#### Hip Strap and Buckle

There is one exception to the "bigger and more comfortable" ethos effected by
the FILBE, the hip strap. The FILBE's hip straight measures 38 mm (1.5") while 
the Kahakka's measures 49.5 mm (1.95"). The material thickness are virtually 
equivalent, but the Kahakka's strap is noticeably stiffer.

Here the FILBE utilizes a standard QR buckle, but the Kahakka employs a Berg
buckle. This is my first experience with a Berg buckle - and I love it. This is
a perfect solution for an item which is supposed to be used during high stress
environments. Even though standard QR buckles can be opened single-handled,
a Berg buckle requires less fine motor control, and can also be easily opened
while wearing winter gloves. The devil's advocate in me does say that a Berg
buckle could be more difficult to unclip in the prone position but I believe this
is more of a chaotic neutral feature (sometimes good, sometimes bad - but equally
so).

#### Compression Straps and Buckles

Both the Kahakka and the FILBE have equivalent compression straps, in number and 
location. The main differences are in the webbing hardware and how excess strap
is organized.

Both packs features dual compression straps on each side. The FILBE only features
dual compression straps on the bottom, but the Kahakka's equivalent straps
compress the bottom, the front, and the top lid. 

The benefit of this later design is more real-estate for extra storaga on the 
Kahakka - either between the straps and the front of the pack, or even beneath
the lid. However, this fact is limited by the relatively short length of the 
straps (69.5 cm or 27.36") relative to the max volume of the pack. Without a
custom adjustment, as will be explained shortly, I was not even able to strap a
3-season duvet sleeping bag (a typical size, smaller than a rugby ball).

An additional concern and area for improvement is that the SR buckles on these 
front straps are too large to pass through the existing PALS webbing slots. This 
means custom adjustment requires removing the SR buckle from the strap, pulling
the strap out of PALS webbing row(s), then reattaching the buckle. The benefit 
of this tedious exercise is more room for attaching items (like a sleeping bag)
that the tension from the PALS webbing doesn't easily permit.

It would be worth while to widen the rows which the compression straps pass 
through to permit the buckles to pass through.

The Kahakka's side straps use G-hooks. They (as far as I can tell) are stamped
metal hooks that have been finished to have soft corners. There is something quite
elegant about them - and yet, I can't help but feel like looking at at ideal
architect's home - beautiful, but impractical. 

From a manufacturing perspective, they're great. Instead of requiring 2 parts like
an SR buckle, there is only 1 piece required. The hook simply connects over a
loop of PALS webbing, which would have already existed there for other functionality.
They're metal and not plastic, so their durability should be even greater than the
SR buckles. But my concerns are in usage. SR buckles can be undone rapidly, and
with one hand, though they nearly always require 2 hands to buckle. G-Hooks are the
opposite. They can be clasped with 1 hand, but <i>always</i> need 2 hands to
unhook. Or rather a hand and a finger, the finger lifting up the PALS webbing to
let the hook slide away.

For combat and stressful situations, I firmly hold the belief that unloading is more important
than loading, and the one handed unbuckling (SR buckle) is more useful than the one handed
hooking (G-hook). Even if one should disagree with this, there is one more thing
to consider. Though the G-hook can be attached single handely, it is very difficult 
to tighten single handedly. Compared to the SR buckles, the adjustment on the 
G-hooks is an exercise in patience. 

I believe Savotta is aware of this drawback, and chose this updated design explicitly
for two reasons. First, the main compartment is acessed via
SR buckles, and not G-hooks. In other words, faster access is provided to the 
main compartment where it might really be needed. I would even bet 2-fingers
of Kyrö whiskey that this was learnings from the Ukraine war as the older M20
military pack features G-Hooks for the main compartment. The second reason is an
inspection of the G-hook reveals that the center bar (that which the fabric 
strap wraps around), is bent in an S-shape, in an orthogonal direction to the 
plane of the buckle. In other words, it creates 2 small, curved rises, and I
believe this improves the the ability for the strap to slide. I don't 
actually know why these G-hooks have this 'S' feature, and couldn't find anything
about it - so if you've an idea, please let me know.

## A Comparative Review with the USMC FILBE Assault Pack (Europe vs America)

**This is a review of the Savotta Kahakka 25L (October, 2024). Unlike most reviews of the pack, this one is critical. Here I compare the new Savotta Kahakka (2024) with my previous backpack, another military assault pack. Feel free to skip the rambling Introduction by going to, "First Impression – and Numbers"**

### Introduction

I've had the same backpack for 9 years (RIP 2015-2024). It's been used at least as many days as I've had it.

Sometimes it hardly carried anything but papers, other times it was stuffed to the brim, camping gear strapped to the sides. It was with me as I rode the métro and walked the streets of Paris to university, it was with me when I reached the foot of the glaciers on Mont Blanc. It's seen the beaches on both sides of the Atlantic, in the USA and Ireland, and beaches on both sides of the Mediterranean, France and Italy, not to forget Corsica in the middle. It's been over the Apennines, Appalachians, Blue Stacks and Derryveaghs, and even graced the Lake District. It's survived a dozen different farms, carried books into at least 4 different universities, and *most importantly*, had a pocket just for holding a fresh baguette.

**This backpack was the FILBE Assault Pack.**

*FILBE Assault Pack with a backdrop of Mont Blanc*

So, any backpack replacing my FILBE Assault Pack (AP) was going to have big straps to fill. And why replace the FILBE AP if it was so cherished?

Simple, 9 years of wear and tear. An early heater accident in a shoddy caravan lead to melted holes, weak points that no patching completely fixed. Eventually, a huge tear formed at the top, becoming so big I stopped using the zipper to access the main pouch. This was in the 8th year mind you. But the coming of the Irish rainy winter coming, and unending annoyance of passerbys letting me know my bag is open (*it is perpetually open because it can't be closed*), encouraged me to move on.

![The rip](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_134519.jpg)
*The rip*

To be clear, I do like reusing things as much as possible. As a point in proof, the FILBE AP isn't even the oldest material item I still use on a weekly basis. That honor belongs to 2 shirts I got in highschool, and are probably old enough to purchase alcohol without me wearing them. Technically the FILBE AP can be patched (at significant expense), but even then, the stitching and the Velcro are unraveling, and the rain-proof zipper covers are wearing off. Patching the top would be like putting new tires on a car with a broken transmission (or battery for future generations).

**Enter the Kahakka 25L**

How does one go from an American military pack to a Finnish one? Well, I enjoy post-apocalyptic exclusion zones filled with mystery and mysticism. Luckily, I came across [The Road to Vostok](https://www.roadtovostok.com/), an indie game (in the making) whose fictional Finnish setting somewhat mirrors that of the Chornobyl and Pripyat Exclusion zones in Ukraine (and similarly is taken advantage of by the [STALKER](https://www.stalker2.com/en) video game series). Recently, Road to Vostok gained its first commercial collaboration, **[Savotta](https://www.savotta.fi/)**, where I learned of the company and its offerings at just the right time.

Savotta is a well-known Finnish supplier of outdoor gear, for both military, police, and recreational use in "extreme-environments."

**Just check out their video from a recent Road to Vostok Post:**  
**["We are Savotta and we make military gear"](https://youtu.be/pOTDmiEPA5A?t=181)**

If that doesn't convince you to buy one of their products (or want to be Finnish), well then, you're clearly not me.

To end this introduction I'll make a few things clear. First, I've never been nor am in the military. I like military packs because for equivalent rugged designs, recreational packs are much more expensive. Specifically, military packs which are GI issued. Recreational packs are also slim lined and generally don't have [PALS webbing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pouch_Attachment_Ladder_System). So, when I discuss military applications and utility, I do so out of with qualified experience. Well…Sort of. My PhD (and previous DIY hobbies) have majorly focused on physical product design and development (including apparel and hardware), so I'm keen to noticing things most people don't. And as ridiculous as it is to say (according to critics), I often built things for the thousands of hours I used to play MilSim games (paintball, airsoft). So, I am familiar with the carrying needs for equipment and the easy access to materials while under stress.

**My usage is everyday**, and I mean, everything that could possibly happen in my daily life which is for better or worse, a lot. I need something that "can do it all." Something that can be loaded up for an overnight hiking trip, or a Joe Six-pack weekend away, something to carry my groceries, something with a hip belt (but no padding), and something that can also be brought to the office, into a museum, and onto a plane (without issue).

**It is a tall ask**, and I am hyper critical

The first time I saw the Kahakka, side by side with the FILBE, I thought, "…and *this* is the difference between America and Europe."

When I go from Europe to America, or vice versa, I get "[aspect ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspect_ratio_(image)#:~:text=The%20aspect%20ratio%20of%20an,3%3A2%20in%20still%20photography.) shock". It's like going from one aspect ratio on your TV, like 16:10 to 4:3 in an instant. Just it's with real life. I haven't heard anyone else get this, but if you do, it'd be nice to know I'm not alone.

When you go to the USA for the first time in a while, it suddenly feels like everything has gotten flattened, smashed down and really wide. The roads are so wide, that a single lane in American suburbia feels equivalent to a rural double lane in Europe. Even the landscape just seems to stretch out further. Meanwhile, go back to Europe and everything has gotten tall and skinny. Go into an older continental European home and you ain't touchin' that ceiling, even if you jump. In Europe, everything does seem skinnier, from the fencing to the sign posts – because it is. You just have to look at American and European printing paper if you aren't yet convinced. Americans love their 8.5″x10″ sheets, and Europeans their 8.3″x11.7″.

If you consider most American products, they are heavier, bulkier, and luxurious (in the sense of comfort). European products are lighter, and maintain economy of material. Cars are a perfect example, even from the same manufacturer. American cars are bigger on average, and when comparing like for like between the same models, just between continents, American suspensions are far softer. While European products are trimmed, this also means they're fitted. You can observe this in how the average person dresses in Europe versus America. Europeans are better dressed. They wear tight, hemmed clothes, pants that aren't jeans, and aren't necessarily blue (or black or khaki), sweaters over their button up long-sleeve shirts, and nice shoes. Oh, and each pair of socks they own are are unique. Not dozens of pairs of the same exact sock. And well….Americans, they wear that one-size-fits-all from Costco, baggy jeans, and if they're a Dad, those classic [608 V5s](https://www.amazon.com/New-Balance-Casual-Comfort-Trainer/dp/B093XKL7WZ) (because hell, *they are* more comfortable).

And that is the difference between the FILBE vs the Kahakka. The FILBE is bigger, heavier, and more comfortable. By appearances, it is baggier. The Kahakka is thinner, smaller, and lighter. It is trim.

## Overview

I will refer to the specifications table above, but write this review in the fashion I examined both packs side-by-side for the first time so it is more story like.

Single Handed Usage (SHU) and Fine Motor Control (FMC) are important concepts in military applications (to me) because they determine how readily a product can be used when injured (single handed usage case) and/or when under extreme stress and fine motor control becomes reduced.

#### Main Compartment

The Main Compartment is the heart of every pack as it will store the most material. Access to the main compartment on the Kahakka is through a Fold Top Lid secured by 2 Quick Release (SR) buckles. The female ends of these buckles are note worthy because they have flared entrances, similar to a [flared magwell](https://gunmagwarehouse.com/blog/the-reasoning-behind-flared-magwells/#:~:text=Well%2C%20it%20has%20a%20larger,truly%20as%20simple%20as%20that.). This is the first time I've seen this on a SR buckle and I can't even get Google Images to show me other like models (I'm sure they exist). This is a fantastic design choice, it really makes buckling easier and in a stressful situation where Fine Motor Control (FMC) goes out this window, this can make a difference.

![Front of pack with 2 SR buckles](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_140513-1.jpg)
*Front of pack with 2 QR buckles and their flared inserts.*

![Plastic Insert on bottom lip](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_140744.jpg)
*Plastic Insert on bottom lip keeps opening squared. Seam on bottom is "rough" where there is a 45 degree in the material.*

![Dark inside the main compartment](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_140758-1.jpg)
*"Hello darkness my old friend…" – Dark inside the main compartment, and elastic water bladder holder*

Unbuckling these and the lid flips open as is common in many recreational hiking packs. Fold Top Lids are great for large recreational hiking packs. They eliminate the weaknesses of zippers while effectively using the pack's shell to keep out the elements. But for an Assault Pack, I'm not convinced they are the right choice. They don't provide easy enough access to materials in the main compartment.

With the Fold Top Lid fully opened, the pack becomes a long tube, too long to effectively reach the bottom. My arm length is equivalent to that of a 6′ or 183 cm man's, and putting the Kahakka on cashier's counter and trying to put groceries in is just not easy. For similar reasoning, larger hiking packs with Fold Top Lids now come with zippered "sleeping bag section" at the bottom. When everything must be packed from bottom-to-top, things will inevitably get pulled out of the pack to get those bottom items. In comparison, the FILBE uses a J-zip, so named because the zipper is stretches longer down one side than the other.

One great design implementation in the Kahakka is a plastic insert in lower fold top lip. This little flexible piece does a great job of keeping the mouth of the lid open and squared (I know it's a rectangle, but rectangled doesn't read as smoothly as a verb). There is however a seam on this lower lip (2nd photo above) which is sharp and seems to catch a lot. It is where the corner fabric I'm very concerned how this seam will hold up in time.

![FILBE Main Compartment](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_134550-3.jpg)

![Kahakka Main Compartment](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_135510-3.jpg)

Inside the Main Compartment, the Kahakka has one single pouch for a water bladder. The top of the water bladder pouched is kept under tension by some kind of elastic band. There are 3 togs for which to anchor the water bladder to, although only the center one is visible in my photo. I am not a fan of elastic tensioning because these materials eventually "dry out," lose their elasticity, and become useless. In comparison, the FILBE uses a buckle strap which will last as long as the buckle does. The FILBE does not have the locking togs the Kahakka does, but this is forgivable because this pouch is intended to hold a military radio and such togs would get in the way. Importantly, after using the FILBE with a water bladder, the lack of togs made little difference in my experience.

Both the Kahakka and FILBE have exit slots for water bladder tubes (or radio antennae). I heavily prefer the flap coverings that Savotta has used. They are located on each side of the flip top lid, just above the hemming in my photos, towards the rear of the packs. The exit slots on the FILBE are covered by Velcro and part of the top carrying handle. Despite rarely using these, they show great wear at this stage. Their top placement makes them less than ideal for inclement weather, but this may be a necessity for the intended radios antennas.

The Kahakka has no other organization in its main compartment. The FILBE has 2 "canteen" pouches. The long one is visible in my photo, beneath the short side of the J zipper. *It was the perfect baguette holder*. There is another "canteen" pouch on the opposite side but it is so small, it is only good for a bundle of socks. I'm not sure what the intended usage was. It seems the perfect holder for a grenade, but also where I wouldn't want to keep one. The FILBE also has a zippered mesh pocket at the top, which I loved. Easy to access, and easy to see what was inside and where it was.

There is one thing which annoyed me greatly about the Kahakka. There is a PU coating on the inside to increase the weather resistance, a great idea, but this PU coating is a hunter's green (i.e. a very dark green). I don't know if this was intentional, so as to reduce any offending light signature that might be produced while looking for or at something in the main compartment at night. But it makes trying to find something in the recess of the compartment, even on a sunny day, a challenge. My constructive advice would have be to use a lighter material to reflect light further down the cavity, and if needed, use a darker coating only at the very top.

One further grievance against the Kahakka is the lack of drainage grommets. These allow water to escape the pack if it manage to get inside. The Finns and the Marines have rather different geographic sectors they are focused on. The lack of grommets may be due to the Finnish Defense Forces pack requirements, which I am wholly ignorant of, and they may have requested no external openings for drainage. With the USMC being focused on maritime operations, it is no surprise there are 5 drainage grommets in the FILBE. Even their [hot weather boot](https://www.bellevilleboot.com/index.php?l=product_detail&p=96) has drainage (although obviously this helps with other issues).

![Bottom of the FILBE](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_134417.jpg)
*Bottom of the FILBE - The FILBE has 5 drainage grommets, 3 in the main compartment (1 visible here, 2 outlines of the others can be seen under the PALS webbing) and 2 in the front pouch.*

![Bottom of the Kahakka](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_140706-1.jpg)
*Bottom of the Kahakka (no grommets)*

**Other Notes:** The FILBE has an additional "compartment" which is the zippered access above the radio/water bladder pouch. This is used for access the rigid plastic back plate of the pack, allowing one to remove it. In the past I've used this pouch to store documents and hide back-up cash while traveling as the zipper goes unnoticed. It is strange that a zipper was incorporated here instead of a flap as it would presumably lower production cost and weight.

**Summary Trade-off (Main Compartment):**

Opening the Kahakka can be done single-handedly with the QR buckles and lid, but accessing materials is restricted and difficult. Opening the FILBE can be done single-handedly, but with significantly more effort. Once opened, materials are very accessible and better organized. The organization on the FILBE is simple, but effective, while the Kahakka has no organization. The Kahakka's internal PU coating may be good for weather-proofing but obscures items in the recess of the bag.

#### Pouches

The Kahakka has 4 additional pouches and pockets. The first is a patch pocket (has its own volume) which is part of the Fold Top Lid, and is accessible from the rear of the pack. This pouch is tiny. Every time I stick my hand inside it is scrapping against the zippers (which are weather proofed by a fabric over hang). It makes me wonder how it's expected for someone wearing gloves in the Finnish winter to readily access anything besides an energy bar from it. Savotta claims, "The one on top of the lid has a capacity of ca. 3 litres, suitable for NVDs, water bottles etc." and their [Kahakka 25L video](https://youtu.be/M3rsKZi5hmE?t=15) also shows them pulling out a pair of binoculars. The shape and accessibility of the pouch is certainly what makes it feel like it isn't 3L large (and I have my doubt about fitting a 2.5L coke bottle in there). And the Savotta video shows you how someone in the Finnish winter will be accessing the compartment: by using one hand to pull the top zipper up.

I'm nervous about putting anything small and important one would normally place here, like keys because the zipper is on the lowest part of the pouch when the flip top lid is closed. Frankly, I've just stopped using it of instead of trying to make it work, but I've no doubt when I get back to hiking and camping something will get stuff inside. The [Medium sized Savotta Rain Cover](https://www.savotta.fi/products/backpack-cover) fits inside but I wouldn't be putting anything else in there with it.

![Patch pocket](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_141023.jpg)

![Flat pocket](/images/articles/design-review/filbe-kahakka/img_20241012_140831.jpg)

The next to cover is the flat pocket on the front. A flat pocket is so named because it doesn't maintain volume of its own, but takes away volume from other places, here, the main compartment. The access is by zipper, again covered by fabric to proof it of the elements, and again it is difficult to access. There is a D-ring inside to attaching items (like keys) with a carabiner or other attachment which is nice. The pocket can just fit a Kindle Scribe (1st generation) which is 19.6 cm wide, and 22.9 cm tall (7.72″x9.06″).

Finally there are the Kahakka famous side pouches. I say this because they are always a surprising treat to every reviewer for the Kahakka as far as I can tell. And yes, they are huge pockets. These are properly 3L!

    hideTimer = setTimeout(function() {
      box.classList.remove('visible');
    }, 200);
  }

  function cancelHide() {
    clearTimeout(hideTimer);
  }

  note.addEventListener('mouseenter', showBox);
  note.addEventListener('mouseleave', startHide);
  box.addEventListener('mouseenter', cancelHide);
  box.addEventListener('mouseleave', startHide);
});
